The on-screen E-STOP is not a safety device Neither is the Arduino, nor the firmware, nor the browser. A USB cable, a browser tab and a Python program can all die in the middle of a move. The only stop you can rely on is removing servo power at the rocker switch. This is a supervised hobby bench procedure.
⚠ 1 known defect in the shipped console — it will lie to you in exactly one place
  1. A bad watchdog value is reported as a bad joint angle. If a watchdog timeout outside the legal range is sent, the firmware answers with a bare ERR E5 carrying no detail [factorylm_arm_controller.ino line 790, errPlain(F("E5"))]. The console's E5 handler is written for a rejected joint angle, so it prints “that starting angle is outside its limits” [arm-console.html, ERR_TEXT.E5]. That sentence is wrong for this case. Correct meaning: the watchdog value must be 0, or between 200 and 10000 ms [.ino line 176]. If you were not touching a joint angle when you saw that message, this is what happened.

1 The 60-second mental model

There are two separate electrical circuits and one wire that ties them together. Nearly every fault on this bench is one of those three things.

   USB  ─────────────────────────►  ARDUINO         (logic only — the brain)
                                       │
                                       ├── D3 ──────► servo YELLOW   (signal)
                                       │
                                       └── GND ──┐
                                                 │   ← THE ONLY LINK
   5 V SUPPLY  ── + ──[ FUSE 1A ]──[ ROCKER ]────┼──► servo RED      (muscle power)
                ── − ─────────────────────────────┴──► servo BROWN
                        │        │
                        └─[ 470-1000 uF ]─┘       ← across + and −, near the servo

The corrected power architecture. [V2 §3]

Circuit one: the USB cable powers the Arduino. That is the thinking part. It is tiny — the Uno's own 5 V pin can supply roughly 500 mA total [V2 §3].

Circuit two: the wall supply powers the servos. That is the muscle. One MG996R can pull up to 2500 mA when stalled [V2 §2] — five times what the whole Arduino can give. This is why the two circuits are kept apart.

The link: one ground wire. Arduino GND to the supply's negative rail, and nothing else crosses between them.

A servo signal is not a thing on its own — it is a voltage measured against ground. If the Arduino and the supply do not share a ground, the servo has no reference to measure against, so it cannot read the signal at all. It sits perfectly still and draws nothing. That looks identical to a dead servo, a wrong pin, or bad code — and it cost this project a full session before it was found [V2, RESOLVED banner 2026-08-01].

And on top of that, a chain of five software links

When the arm console says it cannot connect, the failure is in one of these five, and they fail in this order:

  browser tab  ──►  bridge  ──►  USB port  ──►  bootloader  ──►  firmware
   (the page)     (the black    (COM5, one    (~1 second      (must answer
                   window)       program at    after reset)    NAME=FACTORYLM-ARM)
                                 a time)

The console deliberately refuses to talk to a board that does not identify itself correctly. It opens the port, waits two seconds for the bootloader to finish, then asks VER up to three times. Unless the answer contains exactly NAME=FACTORYLM-ARM, it stops and tells you what it actually found [SERIAL-PROTOCOL §6].

That refusal is a feature. Silently talking to the wrong program on the board is how you end up sending a joint command to a sketch that interprets it as something else entirely.

2 First power-up with the new 5 V / 5 A supply

Do this once, in this order, the first time the new supply is used. It takes about fifteen minutes and it is the difference between finding a wiring error with a meter and finding it with smoke.

Before anything is switched on Set the supply to 5.0 V if it is adjustable, and confirm it with a meter before a servo is connected to it. An adjustable supply left at 12 V destroys every servo on this arm in about a second.

Step by step

  1. Rocker OFF. Supply unplugged from the wall. Nothing energised.
  2. Wire the supply into the barrel jack adapter — the 5.5 × 2.1 mm screw-terminal type. Centre pin is + on virtually every one of these; confirm with a meter rather than assuming.
  3. Fit the 1 A fuse holder in the positive leg, between the supply and the rocker [V2 §3].
  4. Fit the 470–1000 µF capacitor across + and −, physically close to the servo. The stripe on the capacitor body is the negative side. Backwards is a bang [V2 §4a].
  5. Run servo power off the breadboard. Servo + and − go from the screw terminals straight to the servo. Breadboard spring contacts are rated about 1 A at best and degrade with use; a poor contact there produces intermittent faults that look exactly like a failing servo [V2 §4b]. Signal wires through the breadboard are fine.
  6. Fit the common ground — any Uno GND pin to the supply negative rail.
  7. Plug in the supply, rocker still OFF. Meter across the supply output.
    8. between 4.9 and 5.1 V DC. If it reads 6.6 V you are still on the old adapter. If it reads 12 V, stop and adjust before going further.
  8. Rocker ON, no servo connected yet. Meter at the far end of the servo power leads.
    9. the same ~5 V at the servo end. A big drop here means a bad crimp or a breadboard contact still in the path.
  9. Rocker OFF. Connect exactly one servo — the wrist roll, D10, is the right first choice: it is an MG90S with the least mechanical load of all seven [wiring-map.csv, servo type DOC-CONFIRMED], and 5 V is comfortably inside its 4.8–6.0 V range.
  10. Horn off. Tape a toothpick or a paper flag to the spline so you can see small movements [V2 §5].
  11. Rocker ON, then enable that one joint in the console and nudge it.
    12. the flag moves, the board does not reset, and the console stays connected. A reset here means a brownout — check the capacitor and the ground.
One joint at a time, even on 5 A 5 A is enough for several servos in normal motion, but the arm is being reassembled with joints at unknown angles and no measured limits. The reason to go one at a time now is mechanical, not electrical: a joint driven into a hard stop stalls, and a stalled MG996R pulls about 2.5 A on its own [V2 §2].

Same command, different folder. You will want this every time you calibrate a joint on its own:

"C:\Program Files\Arduino CLI\arduino-cli.exe" upload -p COM5 --fqbn arduino:avr:uno --verify "C:\RobotArm\Software\emre_kalem_single_servo_bench_test"

Open the arm console and connect: if the header goes from DISCONNECTED to connected and six joint cards appear, the right program is running. That is a stronger check than any readback.

5 What the refusal messages mean

The controller never fails silently. Every rejected command comes back with a code, and the console turns it into a sentence. Here is what is behind each one [SERIAL-PROTOCOL §7 error table].

CodeWhat it really meansWhat to do
E4 with RESERVED=shoulder_pairYou addressed joint 2. It does not exist as a control — it is the shoulder's second servo, and it is only ever driven together with the first, as J1Use J1. Nothing to fix.
E5 with REQ=… MIN=… MAX=…The angle you said the joint is sitting at is outside that joint's limits. Every joint is still stuck at the 70–110 placeholderIf the joint really is out there, measure it and widen its limits in joint-limits.csv, then press LOAD LIMITS FILE. Do not guess the numbers.
E5 with nothing after itNot an angle problem. This is the watchdog defect from page 1The watchdog value must be 0, or 200–10000 ms.
E6That joint is already enabled, or is not enabled and you tried to move itLook at the card — it says DISABLED or not.
E7The E-STOP is latched. Every enable and move will be refused until it is clearedPress CLEAR E-STOP, then enable each joint again with a fresh angle. This is a common cause of “nothing works any more”.
E9You tried to change limits, speed or the mirror on a joint that is currently enabledDisable it first. You cannot move the goalposts under a driven joint.
E10A row in joint-limits.csv is illegal — limits must sit inside 0–180° and min must be below maxFix the row, reload the file.
E11The shoulder mirror setting is illegal, or the offset would swing the second servo past its travelReduce mirror_offset_deg or narrow the shoulder's limits.
E12Speed outside 1–90 °/sPick a number in range.

Codes E10, E11 and E12 exist because they were once all E5, and the console was telling the operator “that starting angle is outside this joint's limits” for three completely different problems with three different remedies [arm-console.html, comment above ERR_TEXT]. A message that names the wrong cause is worse than no message.

6 It connects, but a servo will not move

Work this table top to bottom. The order is deliberate — the top entries are cheap to check and have actually happened on this bench.

SymptomLikely causeFix / how to confirm
Nothing moves. No noise, no heat, no board reset, no matter which pin Missing common ground. The number one cause on this project One wire: Uno GND → supply negative. Silence with power present is the symptom — a servo with no ground reference draws nothing [V2, RESOLVED banner].
Nothing moves on one pin, but another pin works That signal wire, that breadboard row, or that servo Move the suspect servo's signal wire onto a pin you know works and drive that joint instead. Moves there = the servo is fine and the fault is the wire or the pin. Still dead = the servo.
The board resets or the console disconnects the moment a servo starts Brownout — the supply cannot deliver the startup surge Fit the capacitor if it is not fitted [V2 §4a]. Get servo current off the breadboard [V2 §4b]. Check you are on the 5 A supply and not the 700 mA one.
It moves, but only a little, and stops well short Nothing is wrong. Every joint is clamped to the 70–110° placeholder — 40° of travel Measure that joint's real travel and widen its row in joint-limits.csv. See §7.
The servo turns, but the arm does not The horn is off, or the joint is not bolted to it Expected while the arm is apart. Not a fault.
Something moves, but not the joint named on the card The pin-to-motor map is wrong for that channel See §7 — this is a known open question on D3.
Buzzing, getting hot, not moving Stalled against a mechanical limit, or two servos fighting Rocker OFF immediately. A stalled MG996R pulls about 2.5 A and will cook [V2 §2]. Find the obstruction before re-enabling.
Erratic, intermittent, works then does not A contact — usually a breadboard spring carrying servo current Run servo power on screw terminals straight to the servo [V2 §4b]. Also check the mini-breadboard's +/− rails are not split in the middle; on many they are.
Everything is refused with E7 The E-STOP latch is still set Press CLEAR E-STOP, then re-enable each joint with a fresh angle.
Everything went limp on its own and the console said the watchdog tripped The host stopped talking — tab closed, bridge stopped, cable knocked Reconnect, clear, re-enable. The watchdog is off by default at boot, so this only happens if it was switched on.

The meter test that ends the guessing

With one joint enabled and holding, probe two places [V2 §6]:

ProbeExpectWhat a wrong reading means
Signal pin ↔ GND~0.37 V DC0 V = no pulses on that pin at all. ~5 V = stuck high.
Servo plug RED ↔ BROWN~5 V DC0 V = that servo has no power. Prime suspect.
0.37 V looks like a broken reading and is not. The signal is a 1472 µs pulse repeated every 20 ms — a 7.4% duty cycle — and 7.4% of 5 V is 0.37 V. A cheap meter averaging 50 Hz will wander. What matters is the difference between about a third of a volt, zero, and about five.

7 Three things nobody has measured yet

These are not faults. They are blanks, and the software is deliberately refusing to fill them in for you. Filling a blank with a guess is how a joint gets driven into a hard stop.

a. Every joint's travel limits

All six joints read 70–110° with calibrated=no [joint-limits.csv]. That is the same conservative default the bench-test sketch uses. It is not the travel of any joint on this arm.

To fill one in: load the bench-test program, test that joint alone with the horn off, find where it genuinely stops each way, back off a few degrees from each end, and write those two numbers into its row. Then set calibrated=yes, log it in Calibration_Notes/calibration-log.csv, and press LOAD LIMITS FILE in the console.

Only ever set calibrated=yes for a joint you personally measured A row still sitting at 70–110 with calibrated=yes is a lie the console will believe and repeat, and the amber UNCALIBRATED badge is the only thing that would have warned you [joint-limits.csv header].

The elbow (D6) is the lightest of the four MG996R joints and a sensible one to calibrate first [joint-limits.csv, joint_id=3 notes].

b. Which motor is physically on D3

Two project files disagree, and both are in the repository:

FileSays D3 is
Software/wiring-map.csv, index 0Base — an MG995/MG996R, marked INFERRED
Calibration_Notes/calibration-log.csv, 2026-08-01“the servo physically wired to D3 was the GRIPPER/CLAW”

The console shows this contradiction on the J0 card rather than picking a side. Settle it by size, with no power connected at all: follow the signal wire out of D3 and look at the servo it lands on.

If the servo isThenCorrect
Large, heavy, metal gear horn (MG996R, ~55 g)D3 really is a base-class servo — the wiring map is rightthe calibration-log note
Small, light, plastic (MG90S, ~14 g)D3 is the gripper — the bench log is rightwiring-map.csv and joint-limits.csv, together
Change both files or neither wiring-map.csv and joint-limits.csv use the same joint numbering on purpose, so that this project has exactly one numbering system [SERIAL-PROTOCOL §1]. Correcting one and not the other re-creates the confusion that produced this contradiction in the first place.

c. The shoulder mirror

J1 drives two MG996Rs (D4 and D5) through one link. The firmware refuses to enable it at all until it is told whether the second servo must be given the same angle or the opposite one — that is the E13 refusal.

This is the one measurement never to guess Get it wrong and two MG996Rs push against each other through a single link. They will hold, get hot, and strip a gear. It is the highest-consequence mechanical failure available on this arm, which is why the software removes the ability to express it rather than warning about it [SERIAL-PROTOCOL §1, “Why ID 2 is reserved”].

To measure it: with the shoulder linkage unbolted and both horns off, use the bench-test sketch on D4 and D5 separately, never paired. Note whether the two output shafts point the same physical direction when given the same angle (that is same) or when given mirrored angles (that is inverted). Write it into the mirror_mode column of the shoulder row.

If it turns out to be inverted, there is a second number to measure — the offset. Command each servo until the two shafts point the same physical way; call those angles a and b; then mirror_offset_deg = (a + b) / 2 − 90 [joint-limits.csv header].

Servo horns fit on a splined shaft in whole-tooth steps — about 18° apart on an MG996R — so two horns fitted perfectly symmetrically is the exception, not the rule. If the true mirror axis is 96° and the offset is left at 0, the two servos fight each other by 12° for the entire time joint 1 is enabled.

8 Two faults already paid for — do not re-diagnose these

Both of these cost a working session. If a symptom below matches, go straight to the fix.

1. The servo that had everything and still would not move Power present. Valid ~1472 µs pulses on D3. Twenty-one attach events across seven pins. No current draw, no brownout, no movement. There was no wire between Arduino GND and the supply negative. The complete absence of any electrical event was the diagnostic clue — a servo that is trying and failing makes noise and draws current; one with no ground reference does neither [V2, RESOLVED banner 2026-08-01].
2. “The port is blocked” Only one program at a time can hold a serial port, and four things here want it: the bridge, the Arduino IDE Serial Monitor, a browser tab using Web Serial, and SERVO CONSOLE.bat. A browser tab keeps the port until the tab itself is closed — not minimised, not navigated away from. Closed. The preflight now names whatever is holding it [verified on bench 2026-08-01].

And two tool traps worth knowing

  • Windows reports phantom COM ports from stale registry entries. The port list you can trust comes from the device enumeration, not the registry — which is why the preflight and the bridge both use it [verified on bench 2026-08-01].
  • A program that prints raw text coming back from the board can crash on an odd byte, because the Windows console is not set to UTF-8 by default. The shipped bridge is safe from this — it converts the text properly before it ever reaches a console [arm-bridge.py lines 380 and 493, verified on bench 2026-08-02]. Anything hand-written to talk to the board needs the same care.

Rules that must survive everything

  • Nothing is attached at boot, and no joint is ever centred automatically. Every enable requires you to say where the joint is sitting first, so the very first pulse a servo receives equals its current position and nothing jumps.
  • Limits are measured data. Never invented, never inferred, never copied from another arm.
  • D4 and D5 are one joint. Always.
  • HOLD is not E-STOP. HOLD freezes motion with the joints still powered and holding position. E-STOP cuts the signal — the joints go limp, and a loaded arm sags under its own weight.
  • MIRA-side rule for the whole project: read-only intelligence. This firmware answers; it does not act on its own.
The last word on stopping The rocker switch and the inline fuse are the emergency stop. The button on the screen, the Arduino, the firmware and the browser are conveniences. If something is moving that should not be, reach for the switch — not the mouse.
